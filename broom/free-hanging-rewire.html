Letting the bristles hang free

You want the 136 tube "bristles" to dangle loose at the bottom like a real broom. Right now they're chained in a zig-zag that ties every bottom to its neighbor. Here's exactly what's tying them, how to free them, and whether it's a job you can take on yourself.

The short version

Only the data wiring ties the bottoms — and the data wiring is the safe, low-voltage part of the whole build. The 24 V power is already designed to feed each tube from the top only. To free the bottoms you stop the data from ever dipping down there: run each tube's data back up to the top and hop to the next tube at the top. Your instinct is right; the honest cost is one thin return wire per tube, not zero.

01Why the bottoms are tied today

Each tube is a one-way street for data. The signal enters one end (DIN, data-in), flows through every pixel, and leaves the other end (DOUT, data-out). To light the next tube, DOUT of one tube must reach DIN of the next.

The current layout is a serpentine (a "boustrophedon" — the way an ox plows a field, back and forth). Data goes down tube 1, crosses along the bottom to tube 2, back up tube 2, crosses along the top to tube 3, down again… Half of those crossings happen at the bottom — and each one is a jumper wire physically linking two bristles at their free ends.

02"So only 68 need work, right?" — almost

There are 68 bottom jumpers, and each one links a pair of tubes. So counting junctions, 68 is exactly right. But because every tube sits on one side of a bottom pair, all 136 bottoms are currently tied to something. Freeing them means reworking all 68 junctions, which means putting hands on essentially every tube.

The reason you can't get away with touching only half comes back to that one-way street: to keep all the hops at the top, the leftover signal at each tube's bottom has to travel back up somehow. A straight tube has its two data ends 2.5 m apart, so there's no topology trick that avoids a wire — the signal physically has to climb back up. That climb is the return wire.

The easy thing to miss

It looks like the top-hop between tube 2 and tube 3 leaves tube 3 free at the bottom. It doesn't — because data has to flow through the whole length of tube 3 and then leave to feed tube 4. That top-hop is tube 3's input; its output still exits at the bottom. Every middle tube receives on one end and sends on the other, so it's always tied at both ends. Only the last tube in a chain has a free bottom.

03The fix: bounce the data back to the top

Orient every tube the same way: DIN at the top, data flowing down, DOUT at the bottom. Then, instead of hopping sideways to the neighbor's bottom, run a thin data wire from the bottom back up the hidden side of the tube to the top, and hop to the next tube's DIN up there. The tube's own bottom now connects to nothing beside it — just its own little wire heading up — so it swings free like a bristle.

⚑ Check this on a real tube before you commit

Find the little arrow printed on the strip, or the DIN/DOUT labels, and confirm which end is which. Two cases:

  • Data-in and data-out are at opposite ends (most likely — a normal straight strip): use the return-wire method above. One return wire per tube.
  • Both data ends come out at the top (some "double-sided" tubes are a strip folded at the bottom): you're in luck — just hop top-to-top, no return wires and no bottom work at all. Your tubes are spec'd "double-sided," so it's worth a two-minute look to find out which world you're in.

Here's the crucial split for a first-timer — this project has two very different halves, and they carry very different risk:

The data side — friendly

The rewire you're asking about. Signal is 5 V, currents are tiny. Worst case you mis-wire it and a tube doesn't light — you fix it. Repetitive soldering and routing, but nothing here can hurt you.

The power side — respect it

The 24 V Tesla bank pushes 170–400+ amps. That much current welds tools, melts wire, and starts fires; the battery modules store serious energy. This half is not a beginner-solo job.

05Can you do it on your own?

Yes — for the part you're actually asking about.

The free-hanging rewire is data work: low-voltage, safe, and repetitive rather than tricky. It's a great first real LED project if you learn on a prototype before touching the installed broom. What you should not solo is anything on the 24 V bank, the 250 A breaker, or the busbars — pair with whoever's leading power (the repo has Chris and Mike on that) for the high-current side.

Skills you'll pick up along the way: soldering to fine LED pads, heat-shrink and strain relief, neat wire routing, and driving the tubes from xLights/FPP. None are hard alone; the challenge is patience across 136 repetitions and staying organized (label everything).

A prototype-first path that de-risks the whole thing

Don't start on the car. Prove the method small, then scale — each step is cheap and teaches the next:

  1. One tube on the bench. A small 24 V supply, one controller output, and xLights. Goal: make it light and find DIN vs DOUT, the ground, the series resistor at DIN, and the injection cap.
  2. Two tubes with a return wire. Prove the core idea: data down tube 1, back up the wire, into tube 2, bottoms free. If this works, the whole design works.
  3. One group of four. The real unit of the build (34 groups of 4). Time yourself — this tells you the hours for all 136.
  4. One full controller line. Confirms signal stays clean over the longer return runs and the mapping in software is right.
  5. Roll out to all 136. Now it's just repeating a proven, boring process — the good kind of boring.

A few things to keep in mind

  • Common ground is non-negotiable. The data return only makes sense if its ground reference is shared with the power system. It already is (single ground bus) — just don't break that.
  • Keep the series resistor at each DIN (the ~330–470 Ω you already spec). It tames reflections on the longer return-wire runs.
  • Run return wires on the hidden back of each tube, clipped tidy — out of sight from the viewing side, and short as the geometry allows.
  • Power doesn't change. Each tube is already fed 24 V from the top (single-end injection), so the whole tube — bottom included — stays lit with no bottom power connection. You're only moving data.
